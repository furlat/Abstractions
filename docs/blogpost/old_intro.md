## Introduction





This year was supposed to be the year of the agents, yet they stumble. There have been remarkable improvements in coding agents, but the definitely not sporadic mistakes stand out even more against their skillful successes.
I believe this has mostly to do with our lack of understanding, or better, a lack of framing for what LLM agents actually are. Think of one of the default LangChain or PydanticAI agents interacting with a Python REPL. Informally it is quite easy to interpret what is happening in the successful case: the user inputs an instruction and the algorithm composes the available functions into a program that implements the objective. But under the hood, what is really going on? We generally understand agency as a high-level cognitive process involving high-level abstractions like observations, states, actions, and rewards. On the other side, we understand the underlying language models through the lens of transformers approximating a finer-grained token-level stochastic process. Throughout this blog post we will refer to these two different, yet coexisting, perspectives as the macro-level, or agentic, and micro-level, or autoregressive, view. Our objective here is to contribute a narrative as formal as possible that lifts the autoregression of symbolic sequences into a coherent theory of agentic decision-making.


 We will model the high-level behavior as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) emerging as the optimal decomposition of the token sequence generated by the turn-based interaction between the LLM and the environment. We will see how the original next-token formulation of the transformer in conjunction with special turn-delimiting tokens can be used to express the probability of this sequence as a function of the turns rather than the raw token history. This will give us a clear view of the two distinct problems and their connection. At the macro-level we have a turn-based POMDP with its own action, observation, and hidden state space. The agent observes the history of actions and observations and emits a new action; if the action is valid, the environment updates its hidden state, emits a new observation that is appended to the history, and the process repeats. At the token-level the LLM observes a serialized representation of this history where each turn is separated by a special token and generates a new sequence of tokens; if the generated sequence is valid, the environment emits a new sequence that is appended to the LLM context and the process repeats. The connection is now evident: the macro-process exists when the micro-process is able to generate sequences that are recognized as valid actions by the environment. We can then think of the LLM as an optimizing agent when the micro-level token choice induces high-level actions that lead toward high-reward states.

While the picture is surely complicating, this sort of problem is actually not that novel in reinforcement learning. Think of a robotic arm controlled by a continuous controller which needs to play a videogame using a keyboard. The high level problem is the videogame while the low level problem is the motor control of the arm. This setup is often interpreted as a hierarchical reinforcement learning problem, where a set of local policies generate temporally extended actions modelled as temporal options, while the high level policy choose which option to execute. By the better half of the post I hope to have persuaded you that we can interpret an LLM interacting with an environment as Hierarchical POMDP where the transformer can implement micro-level policies at the level of options which compose into a coherent high level controller. 

Unfortunately, that is not great news, actually quite a bad omen for practical application. The pitfalls are many: from having to discover the set of valid options, to maintaining a consistent belief about the latent dynamics of the environment, to the difficulty of disentangling sources of uncertainty between the two levels with proper credit assignment. In general both hierarchy and partial observability are clear no-goes, unless there is a way to recast the problem as a hierarchical MDP with an already given option space. And that is what we will do in the next blogpost.
